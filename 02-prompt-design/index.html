Section 02

Designing Useful Prompts

A good prompt to Copilot has four parts: who it should act as, what context it's working from, exactly what task to do and what shape the answer should take. Missing any one of these is usually why the first draft misses the mark.

The four-part structure

Template shape
Act as [role]. Context: [what document/situation this relates to]. Task: [exactly what you want done]. Format: [how the output should be structured, e.g. table, list, memo, safety infographic or case study].
This is the skeleton every prompt in this playbook follows. Fill in the four brackets and you'll usually get a usable first draft.

Before and after

Vague version (avoid this)
Help me with a safety report.
Too open-ended. Copilot has to guess the audience, the format and what "help" means, so the output will need heavy rework.
Specific version (use this)
Act as a unit safety officer. Context: I'm preparing a monthly safety report for my commanding officer covering January 2026. Task: draft a one-page summary covering incidents, near-misses, and completed corrective actions. Format: use three headed sections (Incidents, Near-Misses, Corrective Actions), each as a short paragraph followed by a bullet list.
Same request, but the role, context, task and format are all explicit. The output will need far less editing.

Other things that improve results

Ask for options, not a single answer
Give me three different ways to phrase the opening paragraph of this SOP, ranging from most formal to most direct.
Useful when you're not sure what tone you want. Pick the best draft rather than trying to fix a single one.
Ask Copilot to flag its own assumptions
Before answering, list any assumptions you're making about my unit, timeframe, or regulation version. Then answer the task below.
Surfaces gaps in your prompt before you get an answer built on a wrong assumption.
If an answer misses the mark, don't start over. Tell Copilot what was wrong ("too long", "wrong audience", "missing the corrective action column") and ask it to revise. Iterating is usually faster than re-prompting from scratch.
